Why this site exists

TikTok Shop is one of the fastest-growing marketplaces of 2024–2026, and most new sellers only know the 6% referral rate shown in Seller Center. The real cost of an order usually includes FBT fulfillment, return handling and, on creator-driven volume, a commission that the seller sets. Added together on a $20 product, that stack reaches 28.6% — and the first time most sellers see that number is after they have already paid for inventory.

TTCalc exists to put that number in front of you before you source. Five tools: a fee calculator that breaks the platform stack out line by line, a profit calculator that adds COGS, shipping, ad spend, creator commission and returns, a break-even ROAS calculator that converts a margin into an ad floor, a side-by-side TikTok vs Amazon FBA comparison, and an AI product photo generator for the listing itself.

Who builds this

TTCalc is built and maintained by L.D. Studio — an independent developer who sells on TikTok Shop. The site started in 2025 after a product that looked clearly profitable in Seller Center turned out not to be once FBT fulfillment, inbound freight and creator commission were counted properly. The gap was not fraud and not a hidden fee. It was that the dashboard reports what settled, while the decision had been made on a 6% assumption.

How the rates are verified

Every fee number on this site comes from the same place: a single shared rate file that all four calculators read from. That file records three things — the effective date of the rate card, the date it was last checked, and the source. When a rate changes, it changes once, and every calculator and article that depends on it follows.

The verification routine behind each update:

  • Start from the platform. TikTok Shop's own Seller Center fee documentation is the primary source for referral rates, fulfillment tiers, and refund handling.
  • Cross-check independently. Each revision is compared against multiple independent fee write-ups. Where a figure appears in several unrelated sources, confidence is higher; where sources disagree, we say so rather than picking the convenient number.
  • Record the dates. Each calculator prints the rate's effective date and the date it was last verified, so you can tell how current a number is without asking.
  • Log every change. Rate movements are recorded on the rate update log with what changed and what it did to per-unit profit, so an older article's numbers can be reconciled against the current card.

A worked example of this in practice: the $0.30 per-order transaction fee that older TikTok Shop guides still quote ended when the flat 6% referral rate took effect in April 2024 and no longer exists as a separate charge. Rather than leave it in the calculators as a trusted-looking line item, we removed it everywhere — calculators, articles, structured data, and the summary files this site publishes for AI crawlers — and left a note on the rate update page explaining why. A rate that no longer exists is worse than a missing rate, because it is invisible.

What we publish, and what we refuse to

A calculator is only as honest as the assumptions behind it. Four rules follow from that:

[ 01 ]

No unverified precision

Where a figure is published by a vendor, we quote that figure and link to it. Where only a third party reports it, the label says so. We would rather write "the vendor does not publish this" than fill a table with a number that looks authoritative and is not.

[ 02 ]

No invented examples presented as data

Worked examples on this site are constructed from the published rate card with the assumptions stated on the page. They are illustrations of arithmetic, not surveys, and they are labelled as such. Figures presented as survey results are cited with a source.

[ 03 ]

No sponsored tool rankings

Comparisons of third-party seller tools are written without affiliate links of any kind. Where we cannot verify a vendor's pricing because it sits behind a login, the comparison says so instead of estimating it.

[ 04 ]

No promises about outcomes

No tool here finds winning products, guarantees a margin, or predicts a trend. Product research tools show what already sold. A calculator shows what the numbers do under the assumptions you enter. Both are useful for exactly that, and nothing more.

How this site is funded

This matters for trust, so it is stated plainly. TTCalc is free to use, requires no account for the calculators, and carries display advertising to cover hosting and development. There is no affiliate revenue, no sponsored content, and no paid placement anywhere on the site — including in the tool comparisons, which contain no tracking links.

Two consequences worth knowing about:

  • Advertising is disclosed. Ads are served by a third-party network and their placement and privacy implications are described in the privacy policy. Ad slots never appear above the calculators, because a slow-loading ad above the fold would cost you more in time than the ad earns.
  • Nothing you type is transmitted. Every calculator runs in your browser. Your sale price, costs and margins are not sent anywhere, not stored on a server, and not used for anything. Saved products in the profit calculator live in your browser's local storage on your own device. The one exception is the AI photo tool, which necessarily sends the prompt you write to its generation service — that is described on the tool page and in the privacy policy.

Corrections

If a rate here is out of date, or a calculation is wrong, the fastest route is the public issue tracker. Rate errors are treated as urgent, because a wrong fee number is worse than no fee number: it produces confident decisions on bad inputs.

When a correction changes a published figure, the page it appeared on is updated and the change is added to the rate update log rather than being quietly overwritten.

How the calculators are built

All five tools are static pages. There is no application server behind them, which is why they load quickly and work on a phone with a poor connection. The trade-off is deliberate: a calculator that shows its formula and its rate dates is easier to argue with, and disagreement with a number is how the number gets better.

Each calculator exposes its method rather than hiding it inside a single output. The fee calculator separates referral fee from fulfillment and from fixed costs. The profit calculator distinguishes net profit per unit from effective profit per unit, because those differ once returns are averaged in. The ROAS calculator reports both the break-even floor and a return-corrected ROAS, since the number in Ads Manager counts orders that later get refunded.

That structure is the point. A single "profit" number invites trust it has not earned. A breakdown invites you to check the line you disagree with.
