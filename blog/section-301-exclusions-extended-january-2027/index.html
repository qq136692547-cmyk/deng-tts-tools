178 Section 301 Exclusions Extended to January 9, 2027: What USTR Actually Posted

The November 9, 2026 expiry did not happen. USTR posted a notice three days earlier extending every one of the 178 exclusions. Here is the primary document, the operative dates, and what the difference costs you if you price landed cost from an outdated assumption.

By TTCalc Research Team · November 9, 2026 · 11 min read

If your costing sheet still has a line that flips on November 9, 2026, stop before you change a single purchase order: rebuild your landed cost with the duty layers you actually pay, entered by you rather than assumed by any calculator. The exclusion clock did not run out today. It was moved to January 9, 2027. That is not our reading of a press summary — it is the determination in USTR's own notice, quoted below. The rest of this article explains why so many seller guides still say otherwise, which exact dates and HTS headings control eligibility, and how to run both scenarios without hard-coding a single rate.

Quick answer

As of November 9, 2026, the 178 Section 301 China product exclusions did not expire. USTR posted a Notice of Product Exclusion Extensions dated November 6, 2026 whose summary states: “This notice announces the Trade Representative's determination to further extend all 178 current exclusions.” Its DATES line reads: “The modifications announced in the annexes to this notice further extend the exclusions through 11:59 p.m. eastern time on January 9, 2027.” Both annexes make the extension effective for goods entered for consumption, or withdrawn from warehouse for consumption, “on or after 12:01 a.m. eastern time on November 10, 2026, and before 11:59 p.m. eastern time on January 9, 2027.” The article descriptions of HTS headings 9903.88.69 and 9903.88.70 are amended by deleting “November 9, 2026” and inserting “January 9, 2027” in its place. Evidence grade: verified primary. No exclusion is added, removed, or reworded; only the date changes. The practical consequence: a shipment entered for consumption on November 10, 2026 through January 9, 2027 that matches an existing exclusion description keeps its relief, and the next real decision point is January 10, 2027.

1. What USTR actually posted on November 6, 2026

The document is a short Federal Register notice from the Office of the United States Trade Representative, titled Notice of Product Exclusion Extensions: China's Acts, Policies, and Practices Related to Technology Transfer, Intellectual Property, and Innovation. It is signed by Jennifer Thornton, General Counsel, Office of the United States Trade Representative. USTR's own exclusion-process page lists it in chronological order as “Notice of Product Exclusion Extensions for Two Months – November 6, 2026,” immediately above the earlier “Notice of Product Exclusion Extensions through November 9, 2026 – December 1, 2025.” That ordering matters: the December 2025 notice (90 FR 55232, FR Doc. 2025-21671) is the one that set the November 9, 2026 date that so much seller content was written against. It is a previous notice, not this one.

The operative clauses, verbatim

Three sentences in the November 6 notice do all the work, and we quote them so you can check them against the source rather than trusting our paraphrase:

  • Determination: “This notice announces the Trade Representative's determination to further extend all 178 current exclusions.”
  • DATES: “The modifications announced in the annexes to this notice further extend the exclusions through 11:59 p.m. eastern time on January 9, 2027.”
  • Annex A and Annex B (identical wording): “The extension is effective with respect to goods entered for consumption … on or after 12:01 a.m. eastern time on November 10, 2026, and before 11:59 p.m. eastern time on January 9, 2027. Effective on November 10, 2026, the article description of heading 9903.88.69 [and, in Annex B, 9903.88.70] of the HTSUS is modified by deleting ‘November 9, 2026,’ and by inserting ‘January 9, 2027,’ in lieu thereof.”

The decision section also uses the phrase “further extend all 178 current exclusions until January 10, 2027.” That is a boundary description, not a second deadline: it is consistent with relief running through the last minute of January 9 and the full list duty applying to entries from January 10. Where wording and the DATES line could be read differently, the DATES line and the annexes govern — they are the operative customs instructions. If you are filing near either boundary, your licensed customs broker, not a blog post, is the authority on the entry treatment.

What the notice does not change

This is where most of the confusion we have seen comes from. The notice extends the dates and nothing else:

  • No exclusion is added or removed. The count stays at 178 — 164 general exclusions under heading 9903.88.69 and U.S. notes 20(vvv)(i) through (iv), plus 14 exclusions for certain solar manufacturing equipment under heading 9903.88.70 and U.S. note 20(www).
  • No product description is rewritten. Eligibility still turns on the written exclusion description matching your ten-digit HTS classification, not on a supplier's catalogue wording or a broad category.
  • An exclusion removes the additional Section 301 duty covered by that exclusion. It does not make a shipment duty free and does not remove other applicable duties, taxes, or fees.
  • The relief is temporary. The notice does not promise another extension; USTR's decision section says it may continue to consider further extensions or additional modifications.

2. Why “November 9 expiry” is still everywhere

If the extension is a matter of public record, why did so much seller-facing material — including a number of late-October guides that priced an immediate duty jump — describe November 9, 2026 as a hard cliff? The honest answer is a sequence of understandable lags, not a conspiracy, and it is worth laying out because the same lag will happen again at the January deadline.

First, USTR published a different Section 301 proposal on October 26, 2026 (FR 2026-21755, 91 FR 67388). That notice proposed a two-month extension of the suspension of the maritime, logistics and shipbuilding action — the China-linked vessel fees and certain crane duties — to January 9, 2027, with a comment period that closed on October 30. Trade press was explicit that the proposal “leaves untouched 178 separate Section 301 product exclusions that remain due to expire at 11:59 pm Eastern Time on 9 November 2026.” So anyone tracking only the maritime docket had good reason, on November 1, to believe the product exclusions were still expiring. As of November 7, several freight briefings still reported that no final exclusions extension had appeared on USTR's public press page.

Second, the November 6 product-exclusion decision arrived on the last business day before the deadline and was posted as a notice on USTR's exclusion-process page rather than as a standalone press release. USTR's November 2026 press index lists four items; the November 6 item is a readout of Ambassador Greer's call with Vice Premier He Lifeng, and that readout discusses agricultural purchases, rare earths and critical minerals — it does not mention the 178 exclusions or any extension. A researcher checking the press page alone would reasonably conclude nothing had been decided.

Third, secondary coverage cascaded on November 8 and 9. Chinese financial press (Caixin, 21st Century Business Herald) reported the two-month extension on November 9; trade-news aggregators carried it on November 8; a tariff tracker updated its November 2 note on November 8 to say the November 9 expiration “is no longer the deadline in USTR's posted decision.” That tracker also stated plainly that its own November 8 Federal Register check did not identify corresponding published notices, and that it was not describing either notice as already published in the Federal Register. We reached the same limit: we verified the notice as posted on ustr.gov on November 6, 2026, but we could not independently verify a Federal Register publication or an FR document number for it. We are labelling that gap rather than filling it with a plausible-looking citation.

3. Why the distinction changes your landed cost math

An exclusion is not a discount you apply at checkout. It is a customs classification outcome, and the variable that decides it is the date the goods are entered for consumption, or withdrawn from warehouse for consumption — not the purchase order date, the sailing date, the bill of lading, or the delivery date. That single rule is why a two-month shift is worth real money to a cross-border seller with containers on the water: a shipment scheduled to arrive in late December now falls inside the relief window rather than outside it.

The second reason the distinction matters is that many costing models still stack tariff components that should not be there. The current layering we model is MFN base duty, Section 301, Section 232, forced-labour provisions, and AD/CVD where applicable. IEEPA-based tariffs were ruled unlawful in February 2026 and a refund process exists; we deliberately do not model them. Several competing calculators still add those dead percentages on top, which quietly inflates a landed cost by double digits before you have entered a single number. Duty-free de minimis treatment ended globally on August 29, 2025, so there is no small-parcel shortcut around the entry question either.

And the third reason: we do not pre-fill any of these rates. Which Section 301 list applies to your ten-digit HTS line is a classification question for your broker. Press reports describe the re-imposed additional duties as 7.5% on List 4A and 25% on Lists 1–3, but that is a description of the published schedule, not a number any calculator on this site should assume for you. Every rate in the landed cost model is entered by you. A tool that hard-codes a tariff percentage is a tool that will be wrong the next time USTR moves a date — which, as this week demonstrated, it does.

4. A two-scenario worksheet you can actually run

You do not need to predict what USTR will do in January. You need two costed versions of the same SKU and a rule for which one governs a given shipment. Here is the sequence we use.

  1. Confirm the classification, not the category. Give your broker the ten-digit HTS code, the product specification and the exact exclusion description. A supplier's phrase like “electronics accessory” is not an exclusion description. Keep the supporting record with the entry documents.
  2. Price scenario A. Goods entered for consumption on or after November 10, 2026 and before January 9, 2027, with the matching exclusion in force: run the landed cost with the Section 301 line you enter as zero for that excluded line, then carry the resulting per-unit cost into a per-unit margin that shows what the duty line does to your break-even.
  3. Price scenario B. The same SKU entered from January 10, 2027 with the exclusion lapsed. Enter the additional duty that your own classification attracts — do not copy a percentage from a blog. Keep every other input identical so the delta is attributable to the tariff alone.
  4. Run the whole catalogue, not one SKU. A tariff change is a portfolio event. price a full CSV of SKUs under both scenarios in one pass and sort by the margin delta to find which lines are actually exposed. If only four of your ninety SKUs sit under an expiring exclusion, a blanket price rise would cost you more than the duty.
  5. Decide by entry date, then write it down. For each open purchase order, record the expected entry-for-consumption date and which scenario applies. A sailing date does not lock in exclusion eligibility.

5. What to do in the two-month window

A two-month reprieve is not a reason to do nothing; it is a bounded planning horizon. Five actions, in order:

  1. Audit which SKUs actually qualify. The 178 exclusions are concentrated in industrial and medical components, certain electronics parts, pumps, motors and solar manufacturing equipment. Finished consumer goods are largely outside the list. If your catalogue is finished goods, the extension may change nothing for you — check before you reprice.
  2. Ask your broker for the entry plan, in writing. Specifically: when will each in-transit container be entered for consumption, and does the filing assume the January 9 date in the amended HTS descriptions?
  3. Refresh the landed cost on qualifying shipments that were budgeted for a lapse. If you built a November price increase into a listing, unwind it with the same discipline you used to add it.
  4. Build scenario B anyway. Relief is temporary and USTR has not committed to another extension. A shipment leaving late in the year may enter after the window closes.
  5. Separate government charges from commercial freight. The separate maritime suspension and ordinary ocean freight, fuel surcharges, terminal charges and drayage are different things. If a quote includes a “China ship fee” tied to the Section 301 maritime action, ask the carrier which charge it is, whether the quote assumes the suspended fees restart, and whether it will be revised.

One more input is worth checking before you commit inventory: whether the underlying demand is still there. Cost modelling tells you what a SKU must sell for; it does not tell you the category is still moving. 6. Frequently asked questions

Did the 178 Section 301 exclusions expire on November 9, 2026?

No. USTR's notice posted November 6, 2026 extends all 178 current exclusions through 11:59 p.m. Eastern on January 9, 2027, effective for goods entered for consumption on or after 12:01 a.m. Eastern on November 10, 2026. The November 9, 2026 date came from an earlier notice published December 1, 2025 (90 FR 55232).

Which HTS headings are affected, and is the product list changing?

Headings 9903.88.69 and 9903.88.70, together with U.S. notes 20(vvv)(i)–(iv) and 20(www). The November 6 notice changes only the date in each article description, from “November 9, 2026” to “January 9, 2027.” No exclusion is added or removed and no product description is reworded. Your product must still match the written description and the ten-digit classification.

Does a sailing date before the deadline protect my shipment?

No. Both annexes key the extension to goods entered for consumption, or withdrawn from warehouse for consumption, within the stated window. Purchase order, departure, bill of lading and delivery dates are not the test. Ask your licensed customs broker to confirm the expected entry date and filing treatment, especially for entries near either boundary.

Should I add IEEPA tariffs back into my landed cost?

No. IEEPA-based tariffs were ruled unlawful in February 2026 and a refund process exists. TTCalc deliberately does not model them; the layers we model are MFN, Section 301, Section 232, forced-labour provisions and AD/CVD where applicable. If a competing calculator shows a large extra percentage in that position, ask what legal basis it is using.

Do TTCalc's calculators pre-fill the 7.5% or 25% Section 301 rates?

No. Every duty rate is entered by you. Which rate applies depends on your own HTS classification and whether a matching exclusion is in force on the entry date — a classification question for your broker, not a default a webpage should guess. We also do not change calculator defaults when a policy date moves; you change the input.

Is the November 6 notice published in the Federal Register?

We could not verify that. We confirmed the notice as posted on ustr.gov on November 6, 2026 (USTR's exclusion-process page lists it by that date and links the notice PDF). Our check of the Federal Register document index on November 9, 2026 did not surface a corresponding published notice, and a tariff-tracking service reported the same gap on November 8. We are not citing an FR document number for it. Treat the USTR notice as the primary source and ask your broker for CBP entry guidance.
